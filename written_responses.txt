Question 1:
The supervised learning model you used: report its accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity in predicting claims.
Refer to specific line numbers where these things are measured.

We used logistic regression that we wrote from scratch with numpy (claim_model.py).
The weights are learned with gradient descent on log-loss (claim_model.py lines 26-32).
On top of the original features, the model also gets each feature squared and each
pair of features multiplied together (claim_model.py lines 12-17). This lets it pick
up curved effects and effects that depend on a second feature. The output is a claim
probability for each customer.

We trained on all 21 features and tested on the 20,000 customers where
index % 5 == 0. Those rows were never used for training. Counting a customer as a
"claim" when the probability is 0.5 or higher, we got

    accuracy    = 69.3%   (metrics.py lines 14-17)
    sensitivity = 27.3%   (metrics.py lines 19-20)
    specificity = 90.5%   (metrics.py lines 22-23)

All three come from the confusion matrix in metrics.py lines 4-12. The test
predictions are made at claim_model.py line 118, and line 119 calls report(),
which prints them.

For comparison, saying "no claim" for everyone already gets 66.5% accuracy but 0%
sensitivity, because only 33.5% of customers claim. Our premiums use the probability
directly and not a yes/no answer, so we care more about log-loss. It is 0.566 on the
test set, compared with 0.638 for giving everyone the average claim rate. The
calibration table (metrics.py lines 44-54, run at claim_model.py line 121) compares
the predicted probabilities with the real claim rates.


Question 2:
How confident are you that your model has not overfit the historical data? Why?

We are pretty confident that it has not overfit. 

We held back 20,000 customers that the model never trained on. The log-loss was 0.5663 on the 80,000 customers 
it trained on and 0.5657 on the 20,000 it never saw, and lower is better. Those are basically the same number, 
if the model had just memorized the training data it would do a lot better on the customers it already saw, which it
doesn't.

The feature search helps with this too. It used 5-fold cross-validation, so a feature was only kept if it made the score
better on customers that were left out of training, and that is why 8 of the 21 columns got dropped. With the 13 features 
we ended up with, the training loss was 0.5662 and the validation loss was 0.5675, which is very close. The model is also 
very small compared to how much data we have. It only has 104 weights and it learned them from 80,000 customers, so there 
is not much it could memorize. The correction table we put on top was also built only from predictions on
customers the model had not trained on.

What we can't know is how it does on new customers that behave differently from the ones in the historical data, 
since everything above was tested on customers like the ones we were given.




Question 3:
In this assignment scenario, which party is acting according to the principle of maximum expected utililty? The customer? Your program? Both?

Both, but they are using different utility functions.

The customer is risk averse so they are following the logarithmic utility curve from the assignment. They look at
two options, one is paying the premium, which is a small loss they know for sure. The other is going without insurance, 
which is a gamble where they might lose the whole coverage amount in an unfortunate circumstance. They work out the expected 
utility of each and only buy if paying the premium is better. In our code that check is passes_utility_check in pricing.py.

Our program just tries to make the most money on average. For each customer, the expected profit is the premium minus the 
chance they claim times the coverage. Then it picks the group of customers that adds up to the most expected profit and still fits
within the $100 million. Our program treats every dollar the same, so for us the most expected dollars is the most expected utility.
That means we follow the same principle, we just don't care about risk the way the customer does.

Because the customer doesn't like risk, they will pay a bit more than what they would lose on average just to not have to take the gamble.
So if we charge anything between our break-even price and the most they would pay, both of us come out ahead. We charge halfway between those two.
